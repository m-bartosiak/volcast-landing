Because **panels lose efficiency as they heat up** — typically 0.3–0.4% of output per degree above 25°C cell temperature. A cool, crystal-clear May day with cells at 30°C beats a July heatwave with cells at 60–70°C, where the temperature penalty alone can shave 10–15% off. Spring records are physics, not a sign your system peaked early.

## Why this happens

A panel's rated power assumes 25°C cell temperature. In full summer sun, cells run 25–40°C above ambient air — so on a 32°C afternoon they can sit at 65°C, giving away over a tenth of their potential. Spring stacks the advantages: sun already high, days long, air cool, and often cleaner air after rain, plus a breeze taking heat off the roof.

Add the fact that heatwaves often come with haze, and the pattern owners see everywhere from Poland to Australia makes sense: the year's record days cluster in April–June, not in the hottest weeks. It also explains why a breezy 25°C day can out-produce a still 35°C one with identical cloudless skies.

## What to do

1. **Compare like with like** — judge summer days against summer expectations, not against your May record. A forecast that models cell temperature does this automatically.
2. **Check ventilation** — panels flush against the roof with no air gap run hotter; a few centimetres of clearance measurably helps in heat.
3. **Do not "fix" what is not broken** — if a hot day's output matches the temperature-corrected forecast, the system is fine. A gap that appears only on hot days, though, can point at a struggling inverter cooking in a poorly ventilated spot.
4. **Plan self-consumption around real peaks** — spring surpluses are the biggest of the year; that is the season when routing surplus into hot water or an EV pays back most.
