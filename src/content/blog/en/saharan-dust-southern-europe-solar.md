Every so often, mostly in spring and summer, the sky over southern Europe turns milky and the sun goes orange. A plume of Saharan dust, calima as it is known in Spain, has drifted north across the Mediterranean. It reaches Spain, Portugal, Italy, and southern France routinely, and on strong events pushes as far as the UK and Scandinavia. For solar owners it does two very different kinds of damage.

## Damage one: it dims the air itself

Dust suspended high in the atmosphere scatters and absorbs sunlight before it ever reaches your panels. During a heavy event, clear-sky irradiance can fall by tens of percent even though there is not a conventional cloud in sight. The sky looks bright, the forecast app says "sunny," and yet your production sags. That mismatch is the signature of an aerosol event, not a fault.

## Damage two: it settles on the glass

What goes up eventually comes down. Dust settles onto panels as a fine film, and unlike a passing cloud, this loss does not clear at sunset. It stays until rain or a wash removes it. Worse is when dust and rain arrive together: the "blood rain" that leaves a dried mud layer, far more stubborn than dry dust and sometimes enough to need manual cleaning.

## Why ordinary forecasts miss it

Most solar forecasts are built around cloud cover, and a dust event is not cloudy in the usual sense. The sky is clear but hazy. The variable that matters is aerosol optical depth, and it takes a dedicated atmospheric model, such as Copernicus's CAMS, to forecast it. A forecast blind to aerosols will confidently overpredict a dusty day, because as far as its cloud data is concerned, nothing is wrong.

## What it means for you

Two practical takeaways. During an event, expect production below what a "clear" sky suggests, and do not go hunting for a hardware fault. The cause is overhead, not on your roof. And afterwards, keep an eye on your output: a lingering shortfall after the haze clears usually means dust is still sitting on the glass, and a rinse, ideally once the dust-laden rain has passed, will bring it back.

A clear sky is not always a clean one. When the Sahara comes to visit, both your air and your glass pay the price.
